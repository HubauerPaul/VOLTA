Speed first

Speed is about 30% of the screen height (F36). Everything else sits around it, and there are no more than seven values on the Race screen.

Power bar = shift lights

The 20-segment bar across the top can be read without focusing on it. Blue is drive power, green is recuperation left of zero (F15), and amber means boost is active.

Never show frozen values

Every signal has a timestamp. After 3 missed cycles (at most 500 ms) the value turns into “—” and a red banner appears (F104, F105). The dots on the Technical screen show how old each signal is.

Pit messages

Messages from the race engineer appear in amber above the speed and the driver dismisses them with a wheel button. You can try it with the pit dashboard further down the page.

Race engineer monitoring

VOLTA Pit

A browser dashboard on a laptop or tablet in the pit. It uses the same WebSocket telemetry the configuration app uses (F123), so no second backend is needed. The race engineer watches the kart and sends predefined messages. Pit-to-driver messages never change vehicle parameters. Config writes still go through the spec's safety layer (F66–F73). No fixed track

Portable timing & pit hub

Proposal · not yet discussed

VOLTA runs at different venues without a permanent track. Everything outside the kart has to fit in a case, run on batteries and be ready in under 15 minutes. That means two portable parts: VOLTA Gate units on tripods for timing, and a VOLTA Pit Hub for the connection to the pit.

Timing modes

CircuitOne gate is both start and finish. Gives lap times, best lap and a live delta. The layout can be anything: a car park, cones, a hall.
Sprint A→BSeparate start and finish gates. Gives run times, e.g. acceleration or slalom runs. Can start from standstill or with a flying start.
Sectors (optional)Extra gates split a lap or run into sectors. Uses the same gate hardware with a different ID.

Proposal: IR first, light barrier as reference

A light barrier is precise, but on its own it can't tell a kart from a person walking by. It also needs a radio link before the driver sees a time, and with separate start and finish gates the two gate clocks must be synchronised. An IR beacon avoids all three problems, because the kart measures both lines with its own clock. So each VOLTA Gate gets an IR beacon as its main timing, with a light barrier as an optional add-on for reference-grade times. A light-barrier trigger only counts if the kart saw the IR beacon within ±200 ms. The GNSS virtual line needs no extra hardware and serves as a fallback outdoors, and as phase 1 while the gates are being built.

VOLTA Gate, one unit for every role
  • Tripod, weatherproof box, USB-C battery for a full day
  • ESP32 + LoRa radio: reports battery, position and light-barrier events to the hub
  • Coded IR beacon: the gate ID tells the kart whether it is start, finish, S/F or a sector
  • GNSS module: gate position on the map + PPS time for light-barrier timestamps
  • Optional retro-reflective light barrier + reflector on a second tripod

Pit connection that travels

The VOLTA Pit Hub is a case with a telescopic mast (2–4 m, since height and line of sight matter more than transmit power). It needs no mains power and no internet.

  • Wi-Fi 5 GHz access point (outdoor AP or travel router) for full telemetry at 10 Hz, log sync, the VOLTA Pit laptop and phones running the VOLTA App
  • LoRa 868 MHz radio for a small core packet at 1 Hz when the kart is out of Wi-Fi range: speed, SoC, drive state, lap events, alerts, driver messages. Uses the 869.4–869.65 MHz sub-band (10 % duty cycle); verify against the EU rules
  • 12 V LiFePO4 battery (≈ 20 Ah) for a full day, charged at home
  • Optional LTE router to let people watch remotely (a COULD in the spec)

Network roles: the hub is the access point, and the kart, laptops and phones join it. If the kart doesn't find a hub within about 20 s of booting, it opens its own access point (VOLTA-KART-01). The VOLTA App then still works without the hub, e.g. in the workshop.

If the link drops: the kart always logs everything locally. Lap times come from its own IR receiver, so the cockpit never depends on the link. The full log syncs to the hub when the kart comes back into Wi-Fi range.

Coverage check: during the first slow lap, VOLTA Pit colours the GNSS trace by Wi-Fi signal strength. Weak spots show up right away and you can move the mast.

Setting up at a new venue (target: under 15 minutes)

  1. Put up the pit hub on its mast near the pit area and switch it on.
  2. Place the gate(s) at the line(s) and switch them on. They connect to the hub over LoRa and report their battery and position.
  3. In VOLTA Pit, pick the mode (Circuit, Sprint or Free) and assign the gates.
  4. Switch on the kart. It connects to the hub automatically.
  5. Drive one slow out-lap. The trace gets drawn, the gates are checked and the Wi-Fi coverage is recorded.
System

Architecture

Everything runs on the cockpit unit, and the kart works completely offline (F101). The gates and the pit hub are portable and optional: without them, the kart still drives, logs and shows GNSS lap times. The CAN source sits behind a single interface, so the wired bus, the CANlink over Wi-Fi and the simulator (F92, a MUST) can be swapped without touching the decoder or the UI.

Why wired CAN for the cockpit

The spec itself says the CANlink manual warns against using its radio link for anything safety-relevant. Limp home, the MIL and speed are exactly that kind of information. A wired, galvanically isolated tap has one fewer failure point and adds almost no latency. The CANlink is still useful for the phone app and for sniffing from a laptop. Phase 1 can start on CANlink only, since the CAN source interface makes the switch trivial.

Why Qt/QML for the cockpit UI

It renders straight to the display (EGLFS) with no desktop and no browser, which gives the fastest boot and a steady 60 fps (F35, F97). The config app and the pit dashboard stay web-based (or Flutter for the app), because they only need the API. If the team is much stronger in web tech, a Chromium kiosk under cage works too. The catch is 5–10 s more boot time.

For the hardware discussion

Open questions

  • Mounting: where does the display go (steering wheel or column/nose)? This decides 5″ vs 7″ and the reading distance.
  • Power: does the kart have a 12 V auxiliary rail, or do we need a DC-DC from the traction pack? What is the voltage range, and is there an ignition/key signal?
  • CAN access: are we allowed to tap the bus by wire? Where, and at which bit rate? Is the full MAX-Dome DBC available, especially RPM and torque (spec §6)?
  • Venues: will we ever drive indoors (a hall or an underground car park)? GNSS doesn't work there, so indoors the IR gates become mandatory. How far can the kart get from the pit at the biggest venue?
  • Timing: laps only, A→B runs, or both? Is the timing for training feedback (IR is enough) or for official results (add the light barrier)?
  • Several karts: will more than one kart drive at the same time? A light barrier alone can't tell them apart; the IR method can.
  • Conditions: will the kart be driven in rain? This decides whether we need IP65 or IP67.
  • Budget and whether the hardware must be reusable for more karts later (F: “mehrere Karts”).
Suggested first steps
  • Build the CAN signal matrix / DBC from the MAX-Dome document and mark each signal as documented or verified.
  • Record a CAN log on the real kart with the CANlink and a laptop.
  • Build the simulator that replays that log. Then build the cockpit UI against the simulator on a Pi 5 with any HDMI screen.
  • Order one high-brightness display sample and test it outdoors before buying the rest.
